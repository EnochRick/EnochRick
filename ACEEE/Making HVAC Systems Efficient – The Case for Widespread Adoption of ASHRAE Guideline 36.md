# Submitted to ACEEE 2020 Summer Study

## ABSTRACT
In the commercial and industrial sector, market saturation of retrofit energy efficiency measures like LED lighting and variable frequency drives (VFDs) on HVAC equipment is expected in the next few years. This is due to decades of energy efficiency program support for incremental upgrades of building equipment, taking old building systems and swapping in better hardware. Reaching the higher levels of energy efficiency necessary to meet our carbon reduction goals will require a more nuanced approach in which HVAC systems are optimized from central equipment to distribution to terminal devices.  

HVAC control sequences define the programming code that drives system operation. HVAC control programs range from providing intermittent comfort and poor efficiency to delivering relatively acceptable comfort at high energy cost to providing good indoor environmental quality (comfort, fresh air and absence of drafts) with the minimum energy input.   

ASHRAE Guideline 36 (“High Performance Sequences of Operation for HVAC Systems”) is a guide to writing sequences of operation to deliver the optimum performance for HVAC systems, either as part of a retrofit or right out of the box when a new building is constructed. This paper provides an overview of the HVAC control sequences defined in ASHRAE Guideline 36, explains the advantages of implementing the sequences using case studies, and looks at the Guideline’s current state and expected evolution. The paper will recommend approaches that Energy Efficiency Programs can use to drive market adoption of Guideline 36 and will provide an estimate of savings potential.  

## Introduction
In the recent past, energy efficiency (EE) programs have relied heavily on lighting measures to provide cost effective savings across the commercial and industrial (C&I) sector and contribute to meeting program goals. Indeed, for one state’s programs during the 2016-2018 period, 60% of net lifetime electric savings came from lighting measures (MA-EEAC, 2019). This is being driven by the rapid transition to LED technology, which can be used to cost-effectively retrofit existing fluorescent and high intensity discharge (HID) fixtures. However, as the market becomes saturated over the next several years, while building energy codes continue to become more stringent and lighting comprises a shrinking portion of building energy consumption , savings will need to come from other areas. 

One obvious candidate to target is HVAC energy end uses, including space heating and cooling, pumps, and fans. The extensive application in recent years of variable frequency drives 

(VFDs) to more efficiently control pumps, fans, and compressors has yielded considerable savings, but may soon be facing the same issues as LED lighting. In order to continue the drive toward net zero energy buildings and meet our carbon reduction goals, we will need to look beyond relatively simple equipment swapouts and seek more creative and holistic solutions. One area with great potential is the optimization of HVAC control sequences, and ASHRAE’s Guideline 36 provides a path forward for achieving these savings.       

## What is Guideline 36?
ASHRAE’s Guideline 36 is entitled “High Performance Sequence of Operation for HVAC Systems” and the name says it all. This document is a thoughtfully developed decision tree for use in designing high end HVAC control systems. The guideline calls for a high degree of tuning and adjustment as well as design phase planning to ensure the systems are capable of constant improvement over time. 

The purpose of Guideline 36 is to provide uniform sequences of operation for HVAC systems that are intended to maximize HVAC system energy efficiency and performance, provide control stability, and allow for real-time fault detection and diagnostics . Perhaps surprisingly, in over 30 years of electronic and digital HVAC control systems, we have yet to achieve all these objectives as a controls industry standard. While many controls contractors and control systems manufacturers will claim they are able to provide this functionality out-of-the-box, it is rarely achieved for several reasons.

## Why is Guideline 36 Necessary?
Achieving efficiency in HVAC systems is complicated by the fact that every building is custom. As a result, the HVAC systems end up being customized to fit the buildings they serve. Factors that drive this customization include building end uses, local codes, owner requirements, budget, schedule, and contractor preferences among many others. There are many standard components in a building. Ductwork, piping, and electrical infrastructure are all examples of where contractors will install a custom system using industry standard components that any of them can purchase. This yields a system that is often installed by one contractor but has no barriers to being worked on by their competitors over the life of the building. Building Automation Systems (BAS) are an exception here and are a commonly accepted “black box” component in buildings that only one contractor can work on for the life of the system.

BAS contractors can also be the victims of poorly written and vague sequences of operations they receive from the design community. Some design engineers prefer to “leave it to the expert” even though the engineers are required to continue their education and be licensed whereas the controls contractor is not. The engineer is responsible for code compliance and ensuring ventilation rates are calculated properly to adhere to ASHRAE Standards that apply to the building. When the engineer passes this responsibility to the controls contractor, this can result in the development of sub-optimal sequences of operation and a building that may consume more energy than is required or not provide acceptable indoor air quality.

There are technological shortcomings that make Guideline 36 necessary. Even in the year 2020 there are a wide variety of controllers that are being manufactured that are not freely programable. This means they run a fixed program that cannot be changed, which allows for some configurability but cannot ultimately be reprogrammed. This lack of programmability is a barrier to saving energy in the terminal units they control. 

These application specific controllers (ASCs) vary in scope from terminal units such as variable air volume (VAV) boxes to air handlers like packaged roof top units (RTUs). These controllers are cheap and provide one function well but are essentially pre-programed thermostats to varying degrees of complexity. They prevent future improvement in the operations of their equipment because they cannot be reprogrammed. The design engineers are not able to alter the sequence of operations, but merely provide the configuration parameters for the fixed program that may or may not meet all the needs of the equipment and building occupants. These clunky solutions save first cost at the expense of higher energy consumption over the life of the building. Each one has a sequence of operations determined by the manufacturer’s proprietary programming and are not industry standard.

Guideline 36 provides a unified approach regardless of how custom a building’s systems are. This unified approach allows the output of a BAS system’s black box to be standardized, so all stakeholders know what to expect. The sequences of operations provided by Guideline 36 have been vetted by experts around the country via ASHRAE. They are detailed and provide context and guidance throughout the document. This will reduce time required for design engineers while improving the quality of their product. It will also allow BAS manufacturers to still provide application specific controllers that compete better on the market because they will have standardized energy efficient sequences implemented. These standardized ASCs will have less of a black box quality to them, because all of their inputs and outputs are defined in Guideline 36. 

## Market Barriers to Energy Efficiency
The majority of building owners are locked in with the contractor that installed their systems until they pay for a replacement system. This is a cost barrier, since BAS systems are labor intensive to replace and often priced based on capacity. A BAS can run in a building on average for 20 years before replacements are considered. Imagine being locked in like that when you buy a car - you can only get oil changes, light bulb replacements and service done by the dealership. Don’t like the price of their service? You have to buy a different car to have the option of going to another mechanic. That’s how the BAS industry currently works because there are no market pressures to open their systems and to standardize.

The BAS market suffers from a self-imposed closed software dilemma that the computer and networking industry solved in the late 1990s through opening software standards. The computer industry quickly realized that there was a need for code to be open and free for the software industry to improve rapidly over time. The invention of Python, HTML, Java and countless other programming languages are proof that companies can use the same coding language and still make money. These languages generate the same output regardless of the computer hardware manufacturer. This approach has allowed the internet to flourish, and code to become peer reviewed, optimized, and shared for the benefit of the industry. It has allowed talent and invention to drive systems to be better and improve over time.

This is a lesson the BAS industry knows well as they use these very tools in developing their products to build their front ends and their controls networks. However, the most important component to unlocking energy savings in a building, the automation programming languages (the programming customized to each building’s unique sequence of operations), are proprietary and often locked down. The industry thus creates a barrier to energy efficiency because there is no possible peer review, no standard approach, and no way to apply pressure to improve the code.

Furthermore, because the life cycle on a BAS is so long, contractors can sometimes find themselves in a race to the bottom when initially pricing a system, with the expectation that any losses incurred during the first year of construction and subsequent year of warranty can be recovered over the following twenty years. This leads to many contractors installing systems with a lower degree of quality to keep their bids low, and allows for poorly implemented sequences to persist for decades.

Since each BAS manufacturer has proprietary hardware and software that is sold through regionally exclusive dealer networks, the market is designed to prevent local competition. The other controls contractors in an area cannot simply modify the control logic (the code that implements the sequence of operations) of a system installed by their competitor the way a Java programmer can read and modify the code of a competitor’s Java system. They also follow a dealership model that prevents more than one dealer of the same product from occupying a market territory. This prevents local competition from happening in most areas, and is another market barrier to energy efficiency and improving on system capabilities.

## Guideline 36 is Part of the Solution
ASHRAE Guideline 36 is a standard for sequences of operation. It creates a way for both design engineers and controls contractors to standardize on the output of a control system given the same inputs. It provides high quality sequences that are specific and provides a plethora of context and explanation for their complexity. Just as a Java application can provide the same output on MacOS, Microsoft Windows, or Linux given the same inputs on each machine, Guideline 36 attempts to achieve a similar result via standard control sequences and a standardized industry understanding of what the output should be given similar inputs. Of course, the comparison to software alone is a bit unfair since every building is custom. However, this is a major step to unlocking the potential energy savings within these BAS black boxes.

Guideline 36 uses some common approaches to achieve energy efficiency. The guideline calls for control function parameters such as timing in delays, gains in PID loops, deadbands and hysteresis all to be adjustable. This opens up controls systems to greater adjustability and tuning over the life of the building. This step alone allows the black box of a control system to become less opaque as a building owner can now tune their own system without having to call a contractor.

It closes control loops and makes use of sensors commonly installed in all BAS systems. It treats groups of equipment as systems enabling direct zone feedback to directly influence the behavior of air handlers, boiler, and chiller plants. Using the commonly untapped potential of highly networked BAS products, Guideline 36 software utilizes the best of BAS capabilities to get HVAC systems to work together directly to address the needs of the building.

Guideline 36, if successful in market adoption over the decade, should be able to reduce cost, reduce errors, and improve energy efficiency in buildings. This is partly because it will establish industry standard practice and verbiage. By having a common set of sequences to follow, both design engineers and controls contractors will better understand each other and provide more uniform control across the building stock. Building operators will learn these sequences as well and the number of quick fixes should decline as the baseline operations of HVAC is easier to understand and predict.

While peer review of the code within a BAS system will still remain a barrier, the output of that code will become more commonly understood. This will improve local competition since some contractors will adhere to the sequences better than others and be able to provide a high-quality debugged solution in less time. The majority of their time spent in warranty will deal with any customization that will occur to meet a building’s specific needs, but will likely be achieved via tuning and adjusting the parameters indicated in Guideline 36 at the request of the design engineer. This improved experience should help improve building owner and controls contractor relationships and reduce call backs and service calls. 

 It also should improve compliance with ventilation codes, resulting in improved indoor air quality. If the automatic fault detection and diagnostics portions of the guideline are widely implemented, we should see equipment maintenance improve which also helps reduce energy waste.

For these benefits to be realized, controls contractors will need to pre-develop and vet programming to adhere to the guideline. Commissioning agents will have to pre-develop functional performance testing that holds contractors responsible for proper implementation of the sequences. Design engineers will need to embrace change and have an open mind regarding Guideline 36 compliant designs. Over time, all of these changes in industry will become status quo allowing this higher degree of complexity to enable the energy reduction these HVAC systems are able to achieve.

## History and Description
Guideline 36 started as a research project in 2008 sponsored by ASHRAE Technical committee 1.4 (control theory and application) (Hydeman, Taylor, and Eubanks 2015). The research project team included Taylor Engineering, Automated Logic Corporation (ALC), and Facility Dynamics Engineering. The scope of the original research project included air-side heating and cooling for terminal units, and air handling units. It was intended to provide sequences of operations for built-up units or advanced packaged units.

The research project was aimed at addressing many of the issues described above. The team started by gathering several sequences from controls specifications donated by engineering firms, government, and researchers. The research team developed control diagrams detailing the controller hardware input and output points required to achieve the sequences they tested. Then they used ALC’s controls programming environment to write block logic code to test the sequences. This code is still available on Taylor Engineering’s website for review. 

The Guideline was open for public review over three times before being published in the summer of 2018. The ASHRAE committee would meet via conference call once a month and discuss all the changes recommended during the public review period. These were very productive and very impassioned calls that resulted in the polished standard available today. The Guideline is now under constant maintenance which seeks to clarify and add to the current published set of sequences. The committee is in the process of adding plant sequences as the next step forward toward addressing the most common systems in the building stock.

## Overview of Control Sequences
The Guideline in its current form only addresses air-side equipment sequences. That means it provides sequences of operations for air handling equipment, variable air volume distribution systems and fan powered terminal units. The concepts used to optimize these systems are outlined in great detail at the beginning of the document.  

The Guideline provides a lot of insight into ventilation code compliance using ASHRAE 62.1 and California Title 24. This insight might have previously been unavailable to controls contractors who are generally provided the end results of these standards by the design engineering community. By providing this information to these contractors, they are in essence raising all ships and the general level of knowledge behind building ventilation which will allow a greater potential to improve indoor air quality without sacrificing comfort. The more a controls contractor knows, the better their programming becomes in that they can make their template programs more flexible to meet ASHRAE 62.1 and California Title 24 requirements. 

The Guideline provides not only the sequences and the hardware control points required for the equipment; it also provides a lot of context. The document contains separated italicized text in each section of sequence giving the reader the additional information that might not be apparent in a sequence. It also helps the designer make choices based on their equipment configuration and intent. It does this by using bold text to provide direction to the designer using the document to guide their HVAC controls implementation. This bold text sticks out as you read the guideline and provides direction on choices the designer must make in selecting the right sections of sequence text to fit their application. This meta-information regarding the sequence can help provide a lot of guidance to industry professionals adopting this level of sophistication in their designs. It should lower the learning curve for those who are familiar with controls but may not have familiarity with some of the advanced closed loop controls concepts presented.

The document provides instruction on implementing some advanced closed loop controls concepts. Some of these concepts are not new to the controls industry, however they have not widely adopted due to the lack of independent third-party standardization. Now that Guideline 36 has provided that industry standardization, advanced concepts such as trim and respond with weighted heating and cooling requests can become the new normal in all BAS systems regardless of manufacturer or contractor. The Guideline introduces these concepts from the ground up by first defining the zones to which they belong. 

The base layer of any sequence in the Guideline are the zones. They get split into thermal and ventilation zones. Each space in the building has both a thermal zone and a ventilation zone. Sometimes these zones are one-to-one, but sometimes they are mixed. Think of a building with VAV boxes serving offices on a floor. All of the offices on the outside of the building may be split into different ventilation zones, each with their own VAV box. But they may also share a thermal zone, where all the offices on one side may have a common zone of fin tube radiation. 

From there the Guideline provides information on how to group zones together so that occupancy and ventilation requirements can be considered with respect to the air handling equipment. In this the Guideline takes guidance from ASHRAE Standard 90.1 “Energy Standard for Buildings Except Low-Rise Residential Buildings”. Again, this is another area in the industry where the contractors may have been previously unaware of ASHRAE standards that will now become common knowledge and raise the potential for buildings to have decreased energy usage from day one.

Using the generic thermal and ventilation zones guidance informed by the codes and standards the document starts providing sequences for VAV boxes and fan powered box terminal units. It works its way through the most common configurations of these terminal units including constant volume and variable volume series and parallel fan powered box configurations. The last most common terminal unit with sequences provided in the guideline is dual duct VAV terminal units which are still in use in many office type buildings around the country. By addressing the most common terminal units, the sequences make themselves ideal for retrofit controls upgrade projects.

The sequences then address air handling unit equipment (commonly referred to as AHUs). They target the most common AHU equipment in the current building stock starting with multizone AHUs. The standard assumes that the AHU equipment will have modulating heating and cooling applications. Therefore, it does not address direct expansion (DX) cooling directly. However, it is easily adaptable to DX cooling for most experienced design engineers. It also addresses dual fan dual duct heating AHUs and single zone units. 

## Case Study
In 2015 a large hospital in New England was due for a control system upgrade. The hospital had an existing legacy control system that was installed in the mid-1990s before Microsoft Windows 95 had been released. The system’s controls contractor had maintained it for over 20 years which is not uncommon in the BAS industry. In order to work on the original system, the controls contractor had left legacy DOS laptops on site because they had the necessary serial ports and DOS based programming software required to modify the system in 2014. 

The hospital is a 123,500 square foot general care facility including inpatient and outpatient services, emergency services, operating rooms for inpatient and outpatient surgical services, and speech pathology. The upgrade project had to not only replace all the controllers for all of these spaces but also keep the hospital running at the same time. Cx Associates LLC was the design engineer for the project and used ASHRAE Guideline 36 (in public review at the time) as the basis of design.

The Guideline was adapted and used to inform the sequences of operations. Zone groups were established based on thermal and ventilation requirements but also based on space end use types and ASHRAE Standard 170 requirements. As a result, all the zone ventilation rates were recalculated, and the entire building rebalanced after the terminal unit controls were upgraded. All the zones implemented heating and cooling requests up to their parent AHUs. Trim and respond control logic was implemented building wide. Both of these sequences, per Guideline 36, helped close control loops and refine temperature and ventilation control throughout the building while maintaining occupant comfort. ## Savings Potential
There is significant potential for realizing deep energy savings through widespread implementation of Guideline 36. The 2012 Commercial Building Energy Consumption Survey (CBECS) conducted by the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) estimates there are 781,000 buildings in the U.S. that utilize a BAS, comprising over 37 billion square feet (EIA, 2016). The electric energy intensity in these buildings for end uses including cooling and ventilation amounts to 6.4 kWh/sf (EIA, 2019). If only a 10% reduction in these end uses could be achieved through optimizing the BAS control sequences, this would represent an annual savings of almost 24 million MWh and a lifetime savings of 240 million MWh, assuming a 10-year measure life.

Recommended Approaches to Achieve Implementation
There is a challenge regarding how best to effectively harvest the potential savings from implementing Guideline 36, given that this is a relatively new technology with which many engineers and controls contractors may lack familiarity. For retrofit opportunities, many EE programs already support Retrocommissioning (RCx) and Monitoring Based Commissioning (MBCx) projects, and we believe that implementation of Guideline 36 can be rolled into these existing program structures. Programs can target large customers for recruitment and determine whether a site’s existing HVAC systems and BAS allow for the effective implementation of the advanced control sequences outlined in Guideline 36. Similarly, awareness of and advocacy for the Guideline can be incorporated into new construction programs that focus on design optimization. Following implementation, commissioning should be performed to verify the functional performance of the new sequences.

## Conclusion
Guideline 36 presents a unique opportunity for achieving energy savings in commercial buildings for HVAC end uses such as cooling, heating, fans and pumps. Given the large portion of energy usage in commercial buildings attributable to HVAC operation and the fact that easily achievable savings from LED lighting upgrades may be diminishing, it is vital that new avenues be found to meet our energy and carbon reduction goals. The advanced and standardized control sequences described in the Guideline will allow for cost-effective system optimization and corresponding reduction in energy use and improved indoor environmental quality.
